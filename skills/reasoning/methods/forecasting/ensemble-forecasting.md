# Ensemble Forecasting

## Purpose
Combine multiple independent forecasts into a single prediction that is more accurate than any individual forecast. This method exploits the fact that errors from different forecasters or models tend to cancel out when they are diverse and independent, producing a combined forecast with lower expected error.

## When to Use
- When you have access to multiple forecasts for the same quantity or event
- When the individual forecasts are generated by diverse sources (different methods, models, people, or perspectives)
- When the cost of a poor forecast is high enough to justify the overhead of combining multiple forecasts
- When individual forecasts are known to have error but the errors are not perfectly correlated

## When Not to Use
- When you have only one forecast and no way to generate more
- When the forecasts are highly correlated (e.g., three people using the same model and data)
- When one forecaster is known to be dramatically more accurate than the others and ensemble averaging would dilute their signal
- When the aggregation mechanism introduces more error than it removes (e.g., simple averaging when forecasts are on different scales)

## Problem Signals
- The user has multiple estimates for the same thing and asks "which one is right?"
- Different teams or models produce different forecasts for the same metric
- The user describes a forecasting problem where no single method is clearly superior
- The user has access to a prediction market, a survey of experts, or multiple model outputs

## Inputs
- A set of individual forecasts for the same target quantity or event
- For each forecast, its source (method, model, or person) and any known performance history
- The relationships between forecasters: are they independent, or do they share data, methods, or assumptions?

## Procedure
1. **Collect the forecasts.** Ensure all forecasts are for the same target (same quantity, same time horizon, same definition). Incompatible forecasts cannot be combined.
2. **Assess diversity.** Check whether the forecasts are genuinely diverse. Forecasts from the same model with different parameters are not diverse. Forecasts from people who read the same analysis are not independent. Diversity is more important than individual accuracy for ensemble performance.
3. **Detect correlated errors.** If two forecasts are consistently wrong in the same direction, their errors are correlated. Combining them adds less value than combining forecasts with uncorrelated errors. If all forecasts share a common assumption, the ensemble inherits that assumption's error.
4. **Choose an aggregation method.** Options: (a) simple mean — works well when forecasters have similar accuracy and errors are uncorrelated; (b) weighted mean — weight by historical accuracy (inverse of Brier score or mean squared error); (c) median — robust to outliers; (d) trimmed mean — discard the highest and lowest forecasts and average the rest; (e) extremized mean — push the average away from 50% toward the extremes, which improves accuracy for probabilistic forecasts.
5. **Compute the ensemble forecast.** Apply the chosen aggregation method. If the forecasts are probabilistic, aggregate the probabilities. If they are point estimates, aggregate the values. If they are distributions, use the mixture distribution or average the parameters.
6. **Estimate ensemble uncertainty.** The spread of the individual forecasts provides a lower bound on uncertainty. If the forecasts are tightly clustered, the ensemble is confident. If they are widely dispersed, the ensemble is uncertain. Do not interpret a tight cluster as proof of accuracy — it may reflect shared assumptions.
7. **Validate the ensemble.** If historical data is available, compare the ensemble's accuracy against each individual forecaster. The ensemble should outperform the average individual forecaster and ideally outperform the best individual forecaster.

## Output
- A combined forecast (point estimate, probability, or distribution)
- The individual forecasts that were combined, with their sources
- An assessment of forecast diversity
- The ensemble's estimated uncertainty (spread of individual forecasts)
- Validation results if historical data is available

## Strengths
- Consistently outperforms the average individual forecaster and often outperforms the best individual
- Robust to individual forecaster errors: no single bad forecast can dominate the ensemble
- Exploits the "wisdom of crowds" effect: diverse, independent errors cancel out
- Transparent: the ensemble forecast comes with a built-in measure of uncertainty (the spread of individual forecasts)

## Limitations
- Diversity is hard to achieve and harder to verify; forecasters who appear independent may share hidden assumptions
- Ensemble performance degrades when forecasts are correlated or when one forecaster is systematically biased
- Simple averaging can produce a forecast that no individual forecaster believes, making it hard to explain
- The ensemble is only as good as its inputs; garbage in, garbage out applies

## Common Failure Modes
- **Pseudo-diversity**: collecting forecasts from multiple people who all read the same report, producing a tight cluster that reflects one source, not independent judgment
- **Weighting on noise**: weighting forecasters by past accuracy when past performance is largely luck, producing weights that overfit history
- **Ensemble complacency**: assuming the ensemble is accurate because it combines multiple views, without validating against outcomes
- **Dilution of expertise**: including a generic forecaster (e.g., a naive model) that drags the ensemble toward the base rate, which may be wrong for the specific case
- **Ignoring dependence structure**: treating forecasts as independent when they share a common data source, model family, or analytical framework

## Verification
- Are the forecasts genuinely diverse (different methods, data sources, or perspectives)?
- Has the aggregation method been justified (not just defaulted to simple mean)?
- Is the ensemble forecast accompanied by a measure of spread?
- Has the ensemble been validated against historical outcomes if available?

## Combine With
- calibration for assessing the ensemble's calibration and adjusting aggregation weights
- bayesian-reasoning (from probabilistic category) for treating individual forecasts as evidence and updating a prior
- superforecasting for the full methodology that includes ensemble methods
- reference-class-forecasting for adding a base-rate forecast to the ensemble

## Conflicts With
- Approaches that select a single "best" forecast and discard the rest
- Methods that require consensus among forecasters; ensemble forecasting thrives on disagreement

## Example
**Target**: Probability that a competitor will launch a competing product within the next 6 months.

**Individual forecasts**:
1. Market analyst: 30% (based on industry reports and earnings calls)
2. Product manager: 60% (based on insider knowledge of the competitor's hiring patterns)
3. Statistical model: 45% (based on historical launch patterns of similar companies)
4. Sales team lead: 20% (based on customer conversations and competitor sales activity)

**Diversity assessment**: The forecasts come from different data sources (public reports, hiring patterns, historical data, customer conversations) and different perspectives. They are reasonably diverse.

**Correlation check**: The market analyst and statistical model both rely on public data — their errors may be correlated. The product manager and sales team lead use different, non-public sources.

**Aggregation**: Simple mean = 39%. The market analyst has a strong track record (Brier score 0.12), the statistical model is average (0.18), the product manager is new (no track record), and the sales team lead is below average (0.22). Weighted mean (inverse Brier score weights): 0.33 / 0.20 / 0.17 / 0.14 / 0.16, normalized to weights of 33%, 20%, 17%, 14%, 16% = 39%.

**Ensemble forecast**: 39% probability of competitor launch within 6 months, with individual forecasts ranging from 20% to 60%. The wide spread (40 percentage points) indicates high uncertainty.
