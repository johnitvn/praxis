# Strategic Analysis

## Purpose
Assess an organization's competitive position by systematically analyzing its external environment, internal capabilities, and the strategic options available. This is a synthesizing method that integrates multiple analytical frameworks to produce a coherent picture of where the organization stands and what strategic moves are available.

## When to Use
- When an organization needs to formulate or reformulate its strategy in response to changing conditions
- When entering a new market, launching a new product, or responding to a competitive threat
- When evaluating whether a current strategy remains viable given shifts in the environment
- When preparing for a strategic review, board presentation, or investment decision
- When the organization faces a choice between multiple strategic directions and needs a structured comparison

## When Not to Use
- When the organization operates in a stable environment with no competitive dynamics (use simpler planning methods)
- When the decision is purely operational or tactical (use resource-planning or scheduling)
- When the organization is a monopoly with no competitors and no threat of entry
- When the problem is so narrow that a full strategic analysis is disproportionate
- When the organization lacks the data or willingness to conduct an honest assessment of its capabilities

## Problem Signals
- The user asks "what should our strategy be" or "how should we position ourselves"
- The user describes a changing competitive landscape, new entrants, or shifting customer needs
- The user presents a SWOT analysis and asks for help interpreting it or making it actionable
- The problem involves market entry, diversification, vertical integration, or partnership decisions
- The user says "we need to understand our competitive advantage" or "what are our strategic options"

## Inputs
- The organization's mission, vision, and current strategy (explicit or implicit)
- External environment data: market size, growth rates, trends, customer segments, regulatory landscape
- Competitive intelligence: who the competitors are, their strategies, strengths, weaknesses, and likely moves
- Internal capability data: resources, capabilities, core competencies, financial position, technology, talent
- The strategic question: what decision or choice is the analysis meant to inform
- Stakeholder expectations: what do shareholders, board, employees, and customers expect?

## Procedure
1. Frame the strategic question. Be specific: "Should we enter the adjacent market X?" or "How should we respond to competitor Y's new product?" Avoid vague framing like "what's our strategy?"
2. Analyze the external environment:
   - Use PESTEL to identify macro-level trends and forces affecting the industry
   - Use Porter's Five Forces to assess industry attractiveness: rivalry, threat of entry, threat of substitutes, buyer power, supplier power
   - Identify key success factors: what must a firm do well to succeed in this industry?
   - Map the competitive landscape: who are the players, what are their positions, what are their likely moves?
3. Analyze internal capabilities:
   - Use the VRIO framework (Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Organized) to identify which resources and capabilities are sources of sustained competitive advantage
   - Assess the value chain: where does the organization create value? Where are the cost drivers and differentiation drivers?
   - Evaluate financial position, technology, brand, talent, and culture
   - Be honest about weaknesses: a strategic analysis that flatters the organization is worse than useless
4. Synthesize the external and internal analysis:
   - Use SWOT to integrate findings, but do not stop at the list. For each strength, ask: is it a true competitive advantage? For each opportunity, ask: is it accessible to us?
   - Map the strategic position: where does the organization sit relative to competitors on the dimensions that matter?
   - Identify the strategic gap: the difference between where the organization is and where it needs to be
5. Generate strategic options:
   - Generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation, focus (Porter's generic strategies)
   - Strategic moves: market penetration, market development, product development, diversification (Ansoff matrix)
   - Corporate strategy options: vertical integration, horizontal integration, diversification, divestiture
   - Blue ocean options: can the organization create uncontested market space by redefining the value proposition?
6. Evaluate each option against criteria:
   - Fit with external environment: does it address the key success factors?
   - Fit with internal capabilities: can the organization execute it?
   - Competitive response: how will competitors react?
   - Feasibility: do we have the resources?
   - Acceptability: will stakeholders support it?
   - Risk: what could go wrong?
7. Select the preferred strategy and develop an implementation roadmap.
8. Identify the critical assumptions underlying the strategy and plan to monitor them.

## Output
- A structured analysis of the external environment and competitive landscape
- An assessment of internal capabilities and competitive advantages
- A synthesis of the strategic position (SWOT, strategic gap)
- A set of strategic options with evaluation against criteria
- A recommended strategy with rationale
- Implementation roadmap with key milestones
- Critical assumptions to monitor

## Strengths
- Structured and comprehensive: reduces the risk of overlooking important factors
- Integrates external and internal perspectives into a coherent picture
- Uses well-established frameworks that are widely understood and communicable
- Generates options rather than converging prematurely on a single answer
- Explicitly considers competitive dynamics, which many decision methods ignore

## Limitations
- The analysis is static: it captures a snapshot of a dynamic situation
- Framework-dependent: the choice of frameworks shapes the output; Porter's Five Forces, for example, assumes a certain view of industry structure
- Assumes rational competitors and stable industry structures, which may not hold in disrupted markets
- The quality of the output depends entirely on the quality of the inputs; poor data produces poor strategy
- Can produce analysis paralysis: too much analysis, not enough decision
- May overemphasize the current industry structure and underemphasize disruptive change

## Common Failure Modes
- Treating SWOT as the output rather than an input to strategy generation
- Conducting an external analysis without updating it with current data
- Overestimating internal capabilities due to organizational hubris or political pressure
- Failing to consider competitive responses: assuming competitors will stand still
- Generating too many strategic options without a clear framework for evaluation
- Anchoring on the current strategy and treating alternatives as straw men
- Confusing strategic analysis with strategic planning: analysis informs strategy; planning executes it
- Ignoring the possibility that the best strategy is to exit or divest

## Verification
- Check that the external analysis is based on current data, not assumptions from a previous planning cycle
- Verify that the internal capability assessment is honest: are there dissenting views within the organization that should be surfaced?
- Test that each strategic option is genuinely distinct from the others
- Confirm that the recommended strategy is feasible: is there a credible path to execution?
- Challenge the critical assumptions: what would have to be true for this strategy to fail?
- Review the competitive response analysis: would a smart competitor do what the analysis assumes?

## Combine With
- scenario-planning: to test the strategy against multiple possible futures
- game-theoretic-analysis: to model specific competitive interactions in detail
- option-reasoning: to value the flexibility embedded in strategic options
- multi-criteria-decision: to evaluate strategic options against multiple criteria
- risk-analysis: to assess the risks of the chosen strategy
- premortem: to identify failure modes in the strategy before committing

## Conflicts With
- satisficing: strategic analysis seeks the best strategy; satisficing stops at adequate
- design-thinking: starts from user needs; strategic analysis starts from competitive position
- first-principles: decomposes to fundamentals; strategic analysis works at the level of industry structure

## Example
A mid-sized software company faces slowing growth in its core product. The CEO asks: what should our strategy be? External analysis using Porter's Five Forces reveals that the core market is maturing (high rivalry, low growth) but an adjacent market (AI-powered analytics for the same customer base) is growing rapidly with fewer entrenched competitors. Internal analysis using VRIO reveals that the company's unique asset is its deep customer relationships and domain expertise, not its technology (which is commoditizing). The SWOT synthesis identifies that the company's brand trust with existing customers is a genuine competitive advantage when entering the adjacent market, but its lack of AI talent is a critical weakness. Strategic options generated include: (1) build AI capabilities organically, (2) acquire an AI startup, (3) partner with an AI platform, (4) double down on the core product. Evaluation against criteria of fit, feasibility, and risk recommends option 2 (acquire a small AI startup and integrate it into the existing customer relationships). The critical assumption is that the acquired team can be retained and integrated — the implementation plan includes specific retention incentives and an integration timeline.
