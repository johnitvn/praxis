# Completeness Check

Verify that reasoning covers all important aspects — no gaps, no omissions, no unexamined corners.

## Purpose

A consistent argument can still be wrong because it's incomplete. Completeness checking ensures important factors, alternatives, and consequences haven't been omitted.

## Completeness Dimensions

### 1. Factor Completeness

Have all relevant factors been considered?

**Check**:
- What factors are typically relevant in this domain?
- What factors do experts consider?
- What factors would a dissenting stakeholder raise?
- What factor, if included, might change the conclusion?

### 2. Option Completeness

Have all viable options been considered?

**Check**:
- Is "do nothing" a viable option? (It usually is)
- Are there hybrid options combining elements of the considered options?
- Are there options outside the usual range?
- Is there an option that would be obvious to someone in a different role?

### 3. Consequence Completeness

Have all important consequences been considered?

**Check**:
- First-order effects: direct consequences
- Second-order effects: consequences of the consequences
- Side effects: effects on things not directly targeted
- Distributional effects: who benefits and who bears the cost?
- Long-term effects: what happens after the immediate horizon?

### 4. Evidence Completeness

Has all relevant evidence been considered?

**Check**:
- What evidence haven't I looked at?
- What source haven't I consulted?
- What would the other side present as evidence?
- What evidence would I expect to find if my conclusion were wrong?

### 5. Stakeholder Completeness

Have all affected parties been considered?

**Check**:
- Who is affected by this decision?
- Who has relevant information?
- Who has veto power or influence?
- Whose perspective hasn't been considered?

### 6. Risk Completeness

Have all significant risks been identified?

**Check**:
- What could go wrong?
- What's the worst case?
- What risks are correlated?
- What risks are being externalized to others?

## Procedure

### Step 1: Structured Scan

Go through each completeness dimension. For each, ask: "What am I missing?"

### Step 2: Outside View

Ask: "In similar situations, what do people typically forget?"

### Step 3: Devil's Advocate

Ask: "If I wanted to argue that this analysis is incomplete, what would I point to?"

### Step 4: Gap Prioritization

Not all gaps matter equally. For each gap:
- Could filling this gap change the conclusion?
- If yes → high priority gap
- If no → document and move on

## Common Completeness Failures

| Failure | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Narrow framing** | Only considering a subset of factors |
| **Option blindness** | Missing viable alternatives |
| **First-order thinking** | Only considering immediate consequences |
| **Evidence selection** | Only considering easily available evidence |
| **Stakeholder neglect** | Ignoring affected parties |
| **Unknown unknowns** | Not considering what you don't know you don't know |
| **False completeness** | Feeling complete because you've been thorough in one dimension while ignoring others |

## When to Use

- Before finalizing any significant conclusion
- When the problem is complex and multi-faceted
- When the stakes are high enough that omissions are costly
- When stakeholders have diverse perspectives you might miss
